Everything stays matte and printmade — visible lithographic grain in every color field, no smooth digital shading, no gloss, no soft light effects, no glowing outlines. Palette of a 1920s lithographic poster: deep bottle green and near-black forest green dominating the scene, cold petrol teal as the second accent for the evening air over the valley, and warm ochres — the shouted voice itself is a band of warm ochre light and is the only bright thing in the picture — colors laid dark, saturated and velvety, never pale, never dirty.

Never generic cartoon heads.

Now the NEW subject, drawn in exactly that technique — one single emblematic motif, not a narrative scene: a heraldic seal: one single closed ring made of a continuous broad band of warm ochre light, drawn like a ribbon of sound curved round until it bites its own tail, its surface fully worked with dense lithographic stipple. Standing inside the ring, small, dark and centred, the pointed silhouette of one mountain peak with a second lower ridge behind it, and at the foot of the peak one tiny dark figure of a boy with his head tipped back. The ring is the emblem and dominates the block; everything inside it stays simple and bold enough to read clearly when the whole thing is reduced to the size of a coin. No halo, no glowing outlines, no light rays. Deep behind and far below, small and quiet along the bottom of the block: an evenly stippled deep bottle-green field behind the ring, worked dark and velvety, with no horizon, no ground and no buildings — the ring must sit on it like a stamp on wax. Nothing else — generous calm dark space around the central motif.

No text, no letters, no words anywhere — not even illegible squiggles. No signature, no edition number. No watermark, no sparkle or star symbol in any corner. Tender absurd humor, no kitsch. Square 1:1.
