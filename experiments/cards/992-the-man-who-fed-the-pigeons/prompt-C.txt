Study the attached reference lithographs by Adolf Born and imitate their TECHNIQUE precisely — not their subjects. Reproduce: the granular lithographic stipple texture covering every surface, the soft airbrushed color fields, the fine hatched line work, and the whimsical elongated caricature figures. The finished picture must look like it came from the same hand and the same portfolio as the references, at the same level of craft — the stipple laid dense, dark and rich rather than pale, every surface fully worked; do not simplify into a light cartoon line drawing. Everything stays matte and printmade — visible lithographic grain in every color field, no smooth digital shading, no gloss, no soft light effects, no glowing outlines. Print it on clean, fresh, evenly toned pale paper. NO FAKE AGING: no yellowed or browned paper, no stains, no foxing, no coffee marks, no dirt, no torn or deckled edges, no vignetting, no scratches, no creases, no simulated antique print. The lithographic grain is the technique; artificial age is not. Compose it as ONE central rectangular image block with slightly rounded corners, surrounded by a wide clean margin of fresh pale white paper on all four sides — the margin stays completely empty, no marginalia, no decoration. Palette of a 1920s lithographic poster: deep dark midnight blue dominating the scene, bottle green and bordeaux burgundy wine-red as the two main accents, and warm ochres — golden-ochre lamplight, ochre-lit windows, sandy ochre highlights on stone — colors laid dark, saturated and velvety, never pale, never dirty.

The attached face crops define the mandatory facial grammar for any human being that might appear anywhere in this picture, even the smallest silhouette: narrow elongated skulls, a long straight nose continuing the line of the forehead, small almond eyes set close, a small delicate mouth, fine hatching modelling the skin, pale flesh with a rose blush on the cheekbone. Never generic cartoon heads. (No human figure needs to appear in this particular composition — the grammar applies only if one does.)

Now the NEW subject, drawn in exactly that technique — two emblematic symbols in a single wordless confrontation, not a narrative scene: a heavy dark official wax seal, round and thick like a monument, sunk deep into the lower half of the image block — its face bears one crude embossed pictogram pressed into the wax, an open palm with a single stern diagonal bar struck across it (a wordless forbidding mark, no letters, no numerals). Perched calmly on the upper rim of the seal, indifferent to it: one pale grey pigeon, standing with one foot resting directly on the diagonal bar, its head turned away toward the dark sky, at ease. A single small feather drifts down from the pigeon past the seal. Deep behind and far below, small and quiet along the very bottom edge of the block: the dark silhouette of the same small town rooftops with chimneys and a bell tower, one tiny window lit warm ochre, one small street lamp with warm ochre light, all in midnight blue, bottle green, bordeaux and ochre. Generous calm dark negative space fills the rest of the block around the seal and the bird — most of the paper stays empty.

No text, no letters, no words, no numerals anywhere — not even illegible squiggles, not even on the seal itself. No signature, no edition number. No watermark, no sparkle or star symbol in any corner. Tender absurd humor, no kitsch. Portrait orientation 2:3.
